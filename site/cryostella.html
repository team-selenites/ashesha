Cryostella · with Iris

Ice leaves fingerprints on light

Four short steps with Iris: walk past red into colours your eyes can't see, learn to read a spectrum, listen to one, then test your ears.

Why Cryostella? Cryo comes from the Greek kryos, icy cold, and stella is Latin for star. Cryostella, the icy star, is where we look for the marks ice leaves on light from space.

All the spectra here are real SPHEREx data

Colours of light

Your eyes see only a sliver of the colours out there. SPHEREx sees far past red.

Step 1 · Part 1 of 2

Hi, I'm Iris, goddess of the rainbow. Welcome to Cryostella, the icy star, where we look for ice in the colours of light. Each colour has a wavelength, measured in microns. Drag the slider under the rainbow to the right, past red.

Step 1 · Part 2 of 2

Past red, our eyes see nothing, but SPHEREx keeps going, out to 5 microns, in 102 colours. Ice and gas can leave marks in these colours. Press Next to learn how to read them.

Next: Read a spectrum

A micron is a thousandth of a millimetre. The scale stretches the short colours so the rainbow is easy to see.

Step 2 of 4

Read a spectrum

Light carries a fingerprint of whatever made it. A spectrum is how you read it: how bright the light is at each colour.

Step 2 · Part 1 of 3

This graph is a spectrum: how bright something is at each colour. Move the slider under the graph, and the ring walks along the curve.

Step 2 · Part 2 of 3

Now a challenge: find a dip, where the curve drops. Move the ring into it.

Step 2 · Part 3 of 3

You found a dip! Less light arrived at that colour, as if something soaked it up. In a cold cloud, ice can do that, but the team has not checked yet what causes this one. Try another spectrum, or press Next.

Step 2 · Part 3 of 3

You found a bump! More light arrived at that colour. Around a comet, glowing gas can do that, but the team has not checked yet what causes this one. Try another spectrum, or press Next.

Step 2 · Part 2 of 2

No bumps or dips are marked on this one yet, so there is nothing to hunt for. Look at its shape instead: where does it rise, and where does it drop? Then try another spectrum, or press Next.
